Designing for Trustworthiness

An action design research study on AI knowledge retrieval system redesign in a cultural institution — Lauri Lumme, Ulysseus Joint Master's Degree in AI for Business Transformation.

The result

Does a check that is sometimes wrong still help people calibrate? Four members of the Foundation's staff rated ten prepared query/answers each on 21 October 2026. They separated faithful answers from unfaithful ones cleanly and almost none of that separation came from the check.

Discrimination is a within-rater measure: each participant's unfaithful mean subtracted from their own faithful mean, so how generously a person scores cancels out. Individual values ran from +0.50 to +2.25. A rater who simply followed the flag would score zero, because the design crosses the flag against faithfulness. Half the flagged answers were faithful, half the unflagged ones were not.

Ratings as first given. Three were revised upward after the participant read the source panel. Three different people, three faithful answers, no downward revision in the forty. Taking the revised figures instead moves the mean to +1.81.

What they actually used

Each rating was followed by “why that rating?”. The forty answers were coded on one axis: what the justification appeals to.

the source text17
own prior knowledge13
institutional currency4
internal plausibility3
the flag2
citation or presentation1

Thirty of forty justifications rest on the source text or on the participant's own expertise. The source panel carried the work the verifier was built to do: participants opened 124 of the 160 available source entries, and of the item-views where the order could be determined, 33 of 34 read the panel straight down from the first entry to the last.

The faithful answers were received almost uniformly with item means between 4.00 and 4.75. The unfaithful ones ranged from 1.25 to 4.13. What governs trust is not whether an answer is unfaithful but whether its unfaithfulness is visible against the panel.

A verifier that flags violations without regard to detectability is working the wrong half of the problem.

Where the system actually failed

In the same session participants put fifteen questions of their own to the system. Six produced a correct, grounded answer; two were correct silences about things genuinely absent from the corpus; seven were wrong. A follow-up experiment then put five questions through six phrasings each, thirty runs in all, and located the source passage in the index for every one. In 29 of the 30 runs, whether that passage reached the top four retrieved chunks decided whether the answer was correct.

One question was answered correctly by exactly one of its six phrasings. Two conditions had to hold at once: the journey had to be described as an action rather than named as a noun, and the subject's first name had to be left out. Adding “Gösta” pulled a competing passage 0.035 closer in embedding space, against a winning margin of 0.0002.

In a corpus about one man, his own name is the main obstacle to retrieving facts about him.

The same concentration defeats the verifier from the other side. Its proximity check discards query terms appearing in more than 35% of the retrieved sentences which in the case shown on the poster removed Gösta, Serlachius, taide and kartano: precisely the words standing beside the correct year. And the distance threshold meant to trigger refusal sits at a value the observed distances never approach, so the gate could not fire at all.

What follows for design

  • The staff were already calibrated. The design effort went into a trust signal they did not need and largely did not read. The ceiling on this system is not the user's judgement.
  • Visibility beats verdicts. The panel of retrieved passages did the work; a named, deterministic check sitting above it was cited twice. Nineteen of forty next moves went outside the system altogether, to the book, the archive, or a colleague.
  • Fix retrieval before fixing trust. A check can only speak about what was retrieved. When the right passage is not in the top four, every layer downstream is working on the wrong material, and the verifier confirms a wrong answer against the sources that produced it.

Limitations

Four participants at one institution, one Finnish-language corpus of 29 publications. Participants judged prepared answers replayed from logged runs rather than querying live, so that all four saw identical output; the free-use queries were live. Justifications were collected after the rating and after the panel had been opened, so they are stated reasoning elicited after exposure, not intention. Coding was done by one researcher, so no inter-rater reliability is reported. Nothing here distinguishes the individual participants.
